# Keeping the fetched publisher page with each conversion

> Historical research, incorporated 2026-11-20. It is the legal evidence on which [ADR 0035](../adr/0035-render-the-narration-document-on-request-from-the-stored-narration-source-material-with-versioned-frozen-segmentation.md) rests. Cup stores the narration source material for the conversion's lifetime, as recommended, but did not follow the recommendation to keep the fuller page only briefly or only for failed preparations: it keeps the audiobook source material, already stripped of scripts, styles, images, navigation and footers, for the conversion's lifetime as an accepted legal risk, never served and never shared between listeners. The full fetched page and the rendered page are not stored. [R2 storage](../architecture/r2-storage.md) has the current state. [Original](https://github.com/cup-audio/cup/blob/d15357a055a6d26682c9b175efea14bd2ee08930/docs/research/keeping-fetched-publisher-pages.md) on the unmerged branch `research/keeping-fetched-publisher-pages`.

Research date: 2026-11-18, for [issue #480](https://github.com/cup-audio/cup/issues/480), feeding the storage decision of the map [#472](https://github.com/cup-audio/cup/issues/472). Repository state: `main` at `7cf2a405`. Statutes and EU judgments are read from the official texts (EUR-Lex, the Austrian RIS); US opinions from the Caselaw Access Project's copies of the reporters; product and publisher terms from their own pages on the research date. Nothing was probed against a live publisher.

> **This is not legal advice.** It collects what statutes, courts, publishers and comparable products say, and draws inferences from them that are marked as such. No court has ruled on a service like Cup, so every conclusion about Cup is an inference. A lawyer should confirm the option the operator picks, and the answer changes if Cup ever makes an audiobook public (the glossary's planned **public audiobook**) or shares stored pages between listeners.

## Question

Cup would store, per conversion and for as long as the conversion exists (account deletion erases it), the fetched page HTML and the AI-selected article HTML, to re-render the narration document and to debug. What do copyright law (EU and Austria, the US), typical publisher terms and comparable read-it-later and text-to-speech products say about keeping a full copy of the page versus only the selected article text, and what reduces the risk?

## Answer

**No exception in EU or Austrian law clearly covers Cup keeping either copy; the full page is the weaker case, and the selected HTML adds little to what Cup already keeps.** Storing a page is a reproduction ([InfoSoc Art. 2](#eu-and-austria): "temporary or permanent ... in whole or in part"). The temporary-copy exception ends where storage stops being automatic and transient ([Infopaq](#temporary-copies)). The private-copy exception belongs to the natural person, and the CJEU held that a commercial cloud service that actively takes part in making the copies falls outside it ([VCAST](#private-copy)). The general text-and-data-mining exception lets copies be kept "for as long as is necessary for the purposes of text and data mining", which arguably covers the AI selection step but not keeping the page afterwards for re-rendering and debugging, and it falls away where the publisher reserved TDM in machine-readable form ([TDM](#text-and-data-mining)). For press articles from EU publishers, the press publishers' right adds a second rightholder whose right applies to Cup as a service provider, not only to the listener ([press publishers' right](#press-publishers-right)).

**What separates the two copies is how much more than the narrated article Cup keeps, and for how long.** Cup already keeps the sanitized selected HTML in `audiobook.json` and the narration audio for the conversion's lifetime, which is the article text itself. Storing the unsanitized selected HTML beside it changes little in substance. The full page adds works and data Cup has no use for at all: comments and their authors, teasers of other articles, navigation, and the publisher's scripts, which are computer programs with their own reproduction right. Every further work kept, and every month it is kept, is harder to justify under any exception, under the three-step test ([InfoSoc Art. 5(5)](#eu-and-austria)), and under publisher terms that forbid caching or archiving.

**US law is friendlier but not settled for this case.** The listener's command makes the copy, which favours the listener as the maker rather than Cup ([Cablevision](#united-states)), and courts have accepted full stored copies as fair use where the copies served a different purpose and were protected ([Field v. Google](#united-states), [Authors Guild v. Google](#united-states)). They rejected it where the copy let customers consume the work itself and cost the rightholder a licensing market ([TVEyes](#united-states)), which is closer to what an audiobook of an article does.

**Comparable products push the risk onto the user by contract and do not say how long they keep fetched pages.** Readwise Reader, ElevenReader and Speechify all make the user warrant that they hold the rights to what they save or import, take a license from the user, run a DMCA notice process, and (Readwise) end their license when the user deletes the content or account ([products](#comparable-products)). None of them explains how such a warranty works for a public article the user does not own; the clause shifts liability but cannot create rights the user lacks.

**Recommendation (inference).** Store the selected HTML for the conversion's lifetime; it is what the renderer needs and adds no new kind of copy. Do not keep the full fetched page for the conversion's lifetime: keep it, stripped of scripts, styles and comments, for a short fixed period such as the 30 days the debugging need actually spans, or only for conversions whose preparation failed, and never serve it to clients. Keep both copies per conversion, never shared or deduplicated between listeners, and add a notice-and-takedown path. [Risk reducers](#what-reduces-the-risk) ranks these.

## What Cup does today

Source preparation loads each page in a headless browser twice, without and with JavaScript, because publishers refuse non-browser clients ([conversion](../architecture/conversion.md)). Content selection reconstructs the narrated part from the original HTML, and `createNarrationDocument` sanitizes it once; only the sanitized HTML and the synchronization units land in `audiobook.json`, and the fetched page and the selected HTML live only in expiring Workflow step results ([#472 Notes](https://github.com/cup-audio/cup/issues/472)). Every narration document and its audio are visible only to the listener who converted it (or the authorized users of a conversion grant); no audiobook is public yet ([glossary: public audiobook](../GLOSSARY.md#public-audiobook)). Conversion grants are trials, and the glossary anticipates a "future paid plan", so the service is commercial either way.

## EU and Austria

Directive 2001/29/EC ([InfoSoc](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32001L0029)) sets the frame that the Austrian Urheberrechtsgesetz ([UrhG, RIS](https://www.ris.bka.gv.at/GeltendeFassung.wxe?Abfrage=Bundesnormen&Gesetzesnummer=10001848)) implements:

- **Art. 2** gives authors the exclusive right to authorise or prohibit "direct or indirect, temporary or permanent reproduction by any means and in any form, in whole or in part". Writing the fetched HTML to R2 is a reproduction of the article and of every other work on the page. Even an 11-word extract can be a reproduction in part if it expresses the author's intellectual creation ([Infopaq, C-5/08](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62008CJ0005), ruling 1).
- **Art. 3(1)** reserves communication and making available to the public (§ 18a UrhG).
- **Art. 5(5)**, the three-step test, limits every exception to "certain special cases which do not conflict with a normal exploitation of the work ... and do not unreasonably prejudice the legitimate interests of the rightholder".

### Temporary copies

Art. 5(1) InfoSoc (§ 41a UrhG) exempts temporary reproductions that are "transient or incidental", "an integral and essential part of a technological process", whose sole purpose is a network transmission or "a lawful use", and which have "no independent economic significance". The CJEU held that browser screen and cache copies qualify, partly because they are deleted when the user leaves the page or replaced automatically ([Meltwater, C-360/13](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62013CJ0360), paras. 26–30 and ruling). A copy is "transient" only if "its duration is limited to what is necessary for the proper completion of the technological process" and the process "deletes that act automatically, without human intervention" ([Infopaq](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62008CJ0005), paras. 62–64).

Inference: the copies Cup makes while loading and selecting, which Workflows expires automatically, are the strongest candidates for Art. 5(1). A copy kept for the conversion's lifetime so that the operator can debug later is not transient, and its deletion depends on the listener deleting the conversion, which is the "discretionary human intervention" Infopaq excludes.

### Private copy

Art. 5(2)(b) InfoSoc allows reproductions "made by a natural person for private use and for ends that are neither directly nor indirectly commercial". Austria implements it in § 42 Abs. 4 UrhG ("Jede natürliche Person ... zum privaten Gebrauch und weder für unmittelbare noch mittelbare kommerzielle Zwecke"). § 42a UrhG lets someone else make a copy for another person's own use on order, but only free of charge, or for payment only by reprography or handwriting, neither of which describes Cup.

A third party may provide the copying service ([VCAST, C-265/16](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62016CJ0265), para. 35), but Art. 5(2)(b) precludes "a commercial undertaking" providing individuals with "a cloud service for the remote recording of private copies ... by actively involving itself in the recording, without the rightholder's consent" (ruling; paras. 51–52). VCAST also accessed the broadcasts for its users, which Cup does not do for a paywall, but Cup chooses the browser, loads the page itself and decides what to keep.

Inference: the private-copy exception is unlikely to carry a stored copy that Cup makes and keeps, whichever HTML it is. This applies equally to the narration document and audio Cup already keeps, so the question of #480 is the marginal risk of the extra copies, not whether Cup's core is covered.

### Text and data mining

Directive (EU) 2019/790 ([DSM](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019L0790)) defines text and data mining as "any automated analytical technique aimed at analysing text and data in digital form in order to generate information which includes but is not limited to patterns, trends and correlations" (Art. 2(2)).

- **Art. 3** (§ 42h Abs. 1–5 UrhG) covers research organisations and cultural heritage institutions only. Cup is neither.
- **Art. 4** (§ 42h Abs. 6 UrhG) lets anyone reproduce lawfully accessible works for TDM, and "Reproductions and extractions made pursuant to paragraph 1 may be retained for as long as is necessary for the purposes of text and data mining" (Art. 4(2)). It does not apply where use "has been expressly reserved by their rightholders in an appropriate manner, such as machine-readable means in the case of content made publicly available online" (Art. 4(3)); recital 18 names "metadata and terms and conditions of a website or a service". The Austrian text: "Eine Vervielfältigung nach diesem Absatz darf aufbewahrt werden, solange dies für die Zwecke der Datenauswertung und Informationsgewinnung notwendig ist."

Inference: Cup's AI content selection plausibly is TDM (an automated analysis that yields information about which parts of the page are the article), so the copy made for selection can rest on Art. 4 while it runs. That basis does not cover keeping the page after selection for re-rendering or debugging, because neither is the analysis; and the narration itself reproduces the work rather than information about it, so Art. 4 does not cover the narration document either. Where a publisher has opted out, even the selection copy loses the TDM basis.

Publishers do opt out, and in more than one way:

- The New York Times' `robots.txt` comment and its terms prohibit "text and data mining activities under Art. 4 of the EU Directive on Copyright in the Digital Single Market" ([NYT robots.txt](https://www.nytimes.com/robots.txt); [NYT Terms of Service](https://help.nytimes.com/hc/en-us/articles/115014893428-Terms-of-Service) § 4.1(8)).
- derStandard.at's `robots.txt` disallows the whole site for AI crawlers, including user-triggered fetchers such as `ChatGPT-User`, `Perplexity-User` and `ClaudeBot` ([derStandard robots.txt](https://www.derstandard.at/robots.txt), read 2026-11-18). Cup's browser does not identify as any of them, so these rules do not name Cup, but they show the publisher's position on AI agents fetching on a user's behalf.
- The TDM Reservation Protocol declares `tdm-reservation: 1` in `/.well-known/tdmrep.json`, an HTTP header or an HTML `<meta>`; it is a W3C Community Group final report, "not a W3C Standard" ([TDMRep](https://www.w3.org/community/reports/tdmrep/CG-FINAL-tdmrep-20240510/)).

Whether an opt-out in plain-language terms suffices is unsettled. The Hamburg courts dismissed a photographer's claim against LAION under the German TDM exceptions (LG Hamburg, 310 O 227/23, 27 September 2024; OLG Hamburg, 5 U 104/24, 10 December 2025); secondary reports disagree on what the appeal held about natural-language reservations, and the case is reported as pending at the BGH ([Hogan Lovells tracker](https://digital-client-solutions.hoganlovells.com/resources/ai-litigation-case-law-tracker/case/robert-kneschke-vs-laion-e-v/decision/1); [heise](https://heise.de/-11111575)). The judgment text was not read for this page.

### Press publishers' right

Art. 15 DSM (§ 76f UrhG) gives publishers of press publications established in a member state the reproduction and making-available rights "for the online use of their press publications by information society service providers", for two years after publication. The right does not apply "to private or non-commercial uses of press publications by individual users", to hyperlinking, or to "individual words or very short extracts" (Art. 15(1); § 76f Abs. 3 and 5).

Inference: the private-use carve-out protects the listener, not Cup, which is an information society service provider. Storing a full news article from an EU publisher therefore touches the publisher's own right as well as the journalist's, for two years after publication. Storing only selected HTML does not avoid this, since the selection is the article; storing the full page adds the rest of the press publication on that page.

### Program code and other works on the page

The page's JavaScript is a computer program, protected separately under Directive 2009/24/EC, whose Art. 4(1)(a) reserves permanent or temporary reproduction; the TDM exception reaches it (DSM Art. 4(1)) but, as above, not keeping it afterwards. Comments, teasers of other articles and images referenced from the page each have their own authors. Nothing in Cup needs them after selection.

## United States

17 U.S.C. § 107 ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/107)) weighs purpose and character (including commercial use), the nature of the work, the amount used, and the effect on the market for the work.

- **Who makes the copy.** For a remote DVR, the court found "only two instances of volitional conduct": the operator's in designing and maintaining the system, and "a customer's conduct in ordering that system to produce a copy of a specific program", and attributed the copy to the customer. Playback from "a single unique copy ... made by an individual subscriber" was not a public performance ([Cartoon Network v. CSC Holdings, 536 F.3d 121](https://static.case.law/f3d/536/cases/0121-01.json), 2d Cir. 2008). The court decided only direct infringement: the plaintiffs alleged "only ... direct infringement, not contributory infringement, and defendants waived any defense based on fair use". Inference: per-listener copies made on the listener's request fit this reasoning; a copy Cup decides to keep for its own debugging fits it less.
- **Caches and full copies.** Google's search cache was held not directly infringing, impliedly licensed (site owners could opt out with the "no-archive" meta tag, "a widely recognized industry standard"), fair use, and within the DMCA system-cache safe harbour ([Field v. Google, 412 F. Supp. 2d 1106](https://static.case.law/f-supp-2d/412/cases/1106-01.json), D. Nev. 2006). Full scans of books were fair use for search and snippets, stored on servers protected by "the same security systems Google uses to shield its own confidential information" ([Authors Guild v. Google, 804 F.3d 202](https://static.case.law/f3d/804/cases/0202-01.json), 2d Cir. 2015).
- **Where it fails.** TVEyes' "Watch function", which let clients view "up to ten-minute, unaltered video clips", made the package not fair use, because it "deprives Fox of revenue that properly belongs to the copyright holder" and "usurp[ed] a market that properly belongs to the copyright-holder" ([Fox News v. TVEyes, 883 F.3d 169](https://static.case.law/f3d/883/cases/0169-01.json), 2d Cir. 2018).

Inference: an audiobook of a whole article consumes the work, as TVEyes' clips did, and an audio edition is a market a publisher could license, so fair use for the narration itself is uncertain. A stored full page that is never shown to anyone and serves operations sits closer to Field's cache, but a "no-archive"-style signal (NYT § 4.1(5): no caching or archiving) weighs against implied license. The DMCA safe harbour for "storage at the direction of a user" (17 U.S.C. § 512(c)) requires a designated agent and a takedown process; whether a copy Cup fetches itself counts as stored at the user's direction is untested.

## Publisher terms

The New York Times' terms are representative of large publishers ([NYT Terms of Service](https://help.nytimes.com/hc/en-us/articles/115014893428-Terms-of-Service), read 2026-11-18):

- Content is "intended for your personal, non-commercial use" (§ 2.1), and non-commercial use excludes, without consent, using content in "the development or operation of a machine learning or artificial intelligence (AI) system", including "grounding" and "retrieval-augmented generation", and "providing archived or cached data sets containing Content to another person or entity" (§ 2.1).
- Without consent, users may not scrape by automated means (§ 4.1(2)), "cache or archive the Content" except for a search engine's index of links and short snippets (§ 4.1(5)), or perform Art. 4 DSM text and data mining (§ 4.1(8)).

Whether these terms bind Cup is a separate question from copyright: Cup's browser never accepts them, and enforceability of browse-wrap terms against a fetcher differs by jurisdiction (not researched here). Inference: they matter mostly as the publisher's machine-readable and express reservation (DSM Art. 4(3), recital 18), and as evidence of what a publisher would object to. Keeping a full page indefinitely is exactly "cache or archive"; keeping only the article for the listener's own audiobook is the same use Cup already makes.

## What reduces the risk

Ranked by how much risk each removes for its cost (inference):

1. **Keep the full fetched page only briefly, or not at all.** The full page is the copy with no lasting user-facing purpose and the most third-party works; a short fixed retention (for example 30 days, deleted automatically) brings it closest to Infopaq's automatic deletion and Art. 4(2)'s "as long as is necessary", and limits exposure under "cache or archive" terms. Keeping it only for failed preparations shrinks it further.
2. **Strip what Cup never needs before storing.** Scripts, styles, iframes, comment sections and tracking markup are other works or programs and personal data of third parties; keeping only the main-content subtree also serves GDPR data minimisation ([GDPR Art. 5(1)(c) and (e)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)).
3. **Store the selected HTML for the conversion's lifetime.** It is the article Cup already keeps in sanitized form and is what the renderer needs; it adds no new kind of exposure.
4. **Keep copies per conversion and serve them only to the conversion's owner.** Never deduplicate a page across listeners or serve one stored copy to many: Cablevision's reasoning rests on user-specific copies, and in EU law a single copy offered to many users moves towards making available to the public. Revisit everything here before any audiobook becomes public.
5. **Never use stored pages for training or evaluation sets** without the publisher's licence; Readwise states this outright, and NYT's terms single it out. Cup's eval goldens are a related question this page does not answer.
6. **Add a takedown path and designate a DMCA agent.** All three comparable products run one; it is a precondition of § 512(c) and a cheap way to handle a publisher's objection.
7. **Optionally honour machine-readable TDM reservations** (`tdm-reservation`, `X-Robots-Tag: noarchive`, the publisher's `robots.txt`), at least by not retaining the full page for such sites. This does not resolve the narration itself but avoids keeping copies the publisher has expressly reserved.

## Limits

- No court has ruled on a user-triggered article-to-audio service. VCAST, Cablevision and TVEyes are the nearest analogies, and each differs from Cup in ways a lawyer should weigh.
- The OLG Hamburg judgment text, the Instapaper terms and the Pocket support page were not read in full; claims about them come from secondary or search sources and are marked.
- Publisher terms differ; only the NYT terms and the derStandard `robots.txt` were read. Browse-wrap enforceability, Austrian contract law on website terms, and GDPR beyond data minimisation were not researched.
- The research covers storage, not the lawfulness of the narration and audio Cup already produces, which carry the larger copyright question for any article-to-audio service.
